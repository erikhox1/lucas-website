Design Approach

Methodology

Two foundational decisions — geometry type and working fluid — drive every downstream choice in design, analysis, and manufacturing.

Geometry

A 2D planar
symmetric nozzle

Instead of an axisymmetric (de Laval) design, this project uses a planar symmetric nozzle — two mirror-image contoured plates forming the flow passage.

"Modeling and fabrication is much easier and cheaper compared to the axisymmetric nozzle."

The contour can be machined from two smaller material pieces rather than a single large rotational body, the throat assembly is substantially simpler, and plexiglass side walls provide optical access that an axisymmetric design cannot easily offer.

Working Fluid

Why helium?

The choice of working fluid is the enabling technology that makes Mach 14 achievable. As gas accelerates through the nozzle to Mach 14, its static temperature drops dramatically — far below what air or nitrogen can sustain without liquefying.

With a reservoir temperature of 500 K, helium expanded to Mach 14 reaches an exit static temperature of approximately 7.5 K — just above its 4.2 K liquefaction point. Under the same conditions, nitrogen reaches ~12 K, well below its 77 K threshold, causing phase change inside the nozzle. Helium is the only viable working fluid.

Low Liquefaction Temperature

Helium liquefies at only 4.2 K at atmospheric pressure — meaning it remains gaseous even at the extreme conditions inside the shock tunnel.

Higher Mach Numbers

When expanded to Mach 14 from a 500 K reservoir, static exit temperature drops to ~7.5 K — just above helium's 4.2 K liquefaction point. Air and nitrogen would undergo phase change in the nozzle under these same conditions.

Minimal Infrastructure Change

Switching from nitrogen to helium requires only a new working fluid supply. No structural modifications to the shock tunnel are needed.

Process

Design sequence

Each stage feeds into the next, building confidence before committing to the final geometry.

Helium Characterization

Curve-fit thermodynamic properties — pressure, enthalpy, entropy, and viscosity — across the full operating temperature range. These become inputs to the MOC solver and CFD.

MOC Contour Design

A Python solver generates the diverging wall profile using Prandtl-Meyer theory, canceling Mach wave reflections to produce uniform, shockless exit flow.

CFD Validation

RANS simulation with k-ω SST turbulence captures boundary layer growth and confirms the MOC contour produces the target Mach number with acceptable uniformity.

CAD and CNC Machining

The validated contour is translated into CNC toolpaths and milled into a 6061 aluminum block, then assembled into the existing shock tunnel test section.
